Observability and Tracing for RAG

A RAG pipeline that reports only a final answer and a total latency is effectively unobservable. Every meaningful question about its behaviour concerns a specific stage, and aggregate numbers hide all of them.

Per-stage tracing is the foundation. A single request should emit a span for query rewriting, retrieval, reranking, prompt construction, and generation, each with its own duration. This converts a vague complaint that the system is slow into a specific observation that reranking consumes two hundred milliseconds of a three hundred millisecond budget, which points directly at the fix.

Latency distributions matter more than averages. Mean latency is dominated by the common case and conceals the tail where users actually suffer. Tracking the ninety-fifth and ninety-ninth percentiles per stage exposes problems that averages smooth away entirely, such as a reranker that is fast except when candidate passages are unusually long.

Retrieval traces should record what was retrieved, not merely how long it took. Storing the chunk identifiers, their scores, and their ranks for every query makes it possible to answer after the fact whether a bad answer was caused by the right passage never being retrieved or by the model ignoring a passage it did receive. Without this record, that question is unanswerable and the two failure modes are indistinguishable.

Token accounting turns cost into an engineering metric. Recording prompt and completion tokens per request, attributed to the stage that produced them, reveals that retrieving twenty passages instead of five triples spend for a marginal quality gain.

User feedback closes the loop. A thumbs-up or thumbs-down attached to a stored trace slowly accumulates into a labelled dataset that can be folded back into the golden evaluation set, turning production traffic into the regression suite.

The practical implication for design is that timing and trace context should travel with the response object rather than being written only to logs. A caller that can see where its own request spent its time can act on that information, and a debugging session that requires correlating log lines by timestamp is a session that mostly will not happen.
