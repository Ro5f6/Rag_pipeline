Fine-Tuning Compared With Retrieval

Fine-tuning and retrieval are routinely presented as alternatives to the same problem. They solve different problems, and choosing between them by cost or fashion rather than by problem shape produces systems that fail in predictable ways.

Retrieval supplies knowledge. It is the correct choice when the model needs access to facts it was never trained on, when those facts change, when answers must cite a source, or when access to information differs by user. Updating knowledge means re-indexing a document, which takes seconds and requires no training run.

Fine-tuning shapes behaviour. It is the correct choice for teaching a consistent output format, a domain-specific tone, a classification scheme, or a structured extraction task the base model performs unreliably. It teaches the model how to respond, not what is true today.

The failure mode of using fine-tuning for knowledge is severe. Facts absorbed into weights cannot be updated without retraining, cannot be cited, cannot be access-controlled per user, and cannot be removed on request. A model fine-tuned on last quarter's documentation will confidently state last quarter's facts forever, with no mechanism to detect that it is wrong.

The failure mode of using retrieval for behaviour is milder but real. Enforcing a rigid output format through retrieved examples consumes context on every request and still produces inconsistent adherence, where a small amount of fine-tuning would have made the behaviour reliable and free at inference time.

The two compose well. A model fine-tuned to follow a citation format precisely, combined with retrieval supplying the current documents, outperforms either approach alone.

The practical sequencing advice is to exhaust prompting first, then retrieval, then fine-tuning, in that order of increasing cost and decreasing reversibility. Most problems that appear to require fine-tuning turn out to be retrieval problems, and most that appear to require retrieval turn out to be prompt problems.
